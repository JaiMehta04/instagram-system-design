TL;DR
  • Functional requirements say what the system does (post a photo, see a feed). Non-functional requirements say how well it does it (scale, latency, availability, durability, consistency, security, cost).
  • Prioritise ruthlessly: for Instagram, core = post, follow, feed, like/comment, profiles; extended = Stories, search, DMs, notifications, Explore; out of scope = ads, shopping, live, payments.
  • Working assumptions for this guide: 2B MAU, 500M DAU, ~100M posts/day, feed p99 < ~300 ms server-side, 99.99% availability (≈ 52 min downtime/year), media durability of 11 nines, read-heavy (~100:1 content views to uploads).
  • Consistency is chosen per feature: strong for auth and username uniqueness, ordered for DMs, eventual for feeds, likes and follower counts.
  • Non-functional requirements become measurable SLIs and SLOs, and every requirement should map to a concrete design decision and component. Change a requirement (E2EE DMs, chronological feed) and the design changes.

1. The problem: why requirements come first

"Design Instagram" could mean a weekend photo-sharing app for 1,000 people or a planet-scale platform for two billion. Those two systems share a name and almost nothing else: the first runs on one server with one database; the second needs CDNs, sharded databases, event streams, caches, ML pipelines and multiple data centres. Every architectural decision depends on the requirements. Without them, you can't tell whether a design is good — only whether it's elaborate.

Requirements also protect you from the two classic failure modes:

  • Under-engineering — a single PostgreSQL instance serving 300,000 feed reads per second. It will fall over.
  • Over-engineering — globally replicated consensus databases for like counts that nobody needs to be exact. It works, but it is slow, costly and complex for no benefit.

In an interview the first five minutes are spent here (see How to Approach). In real companies this phase is a design document's "Goals / Non-goals" section, and it is where most expensive mistakes are either made or avoided.

🧠 Analogy Ordering a custom-built car. The functional requirements are "seats five, has a boot, drives on roads". The non-functional ones are what really shape it: "0–100 km/h in under 5 seconds" (performance), "must start at −30 °C" (reliability), "must survive a 60 km/h crash" (durability/safety), "fuel cost under X per km" (cost). A family hatchback and a race car both satisfy the functional list — the non-functional list is what makes them completely different machines.

2. Functional vs non-functional requirements

Functional requirements decide which components exist; non-functional requirements decide how those components are built and scaled.

A useful trick: functional requirements are about one user's experience; non-functional requirements are about all users at once — at peak, during failures, over years.

3.2 Write them as user stories with acceptance criteria

Turning features into precise statements exposes hidden requirements:

As a user, I can publish a post with 1–10 photos/videos and a caption.
  - Upload succeeds on flaky mobile networks (resumable, retry-safe).
  - The post shows on my profile immediately after I publish it (read-your-writes).
  - Followers see it in their feeds within ~seconds to a minute.
  - Videos may show "processing" for a short time; photos appear immediately.

As a user, I can open my home feed and scroll.
  - First page renders quickly; next pages load before I reach them (prefetch).
  - Ranked by relevance, mostly recent posts from accounts I follow.
  - Never shows posts from accounts that blocked me or that I can't access.

As a user, I can like a post.
  - Tapping like twice does not create two likes (idempotent).
  - The heart turns red instantly; the count may lag slightly.

Each acceptance criterion quietly introduces a non-functional requirement (resumability, read-your-writes, freshness, privacy, idempotency) — exactly the material for the next section.

3.3 Functional requirements that people forget

  • Deletion and editing — deleting a post must remove it from feeds, caches, search and (eventually) storage and the CDN.
  • Privacy controls — private accounts, blocking, muting, "close friends" Stories audiences.
  • Pagination — every list (feed, comments, followers) is unbounded and must be paged.
  • Multi-device — the same account on a phone, a tablet and the web; state must sync.
  • Internationalisation — Unicode usernames and captions, right-to-left scripts, local time zones.

4.2 Availability: 99.99% and what it really means

Availability is the fraction of time (or of requests) for which the system works correctly. It is usually expressed in "nines":

TargetDowntime per yearper monthper day
99.9% ("three nines")≈ 8.8 hours≈ 43.8 minutes≈ 86 seconds
99.95%≈ 4.4 hours≈ 21.9 minutes≈ 43 seconds
99.99% ("four nines")≈ 52.6 minutes≈ 4.4 minutes≈ 8.6 seconds
99.999% ("five nines")≈ 5.3 minutes≈ 26 seconds≈ 0.9 seconds

For Instagram we target 99.99% for the core read path (feed, profiles, media) and for uploads. Four minutes per month is far less than the time it takes a human to be paged, log in and diagnose a problem — so four nines cannot be achieved by humans fixing things. It requires automatic failover, redundancy in every layer, gradual rollouts with automatic rollback, and graceful degradation. Extended features such as search or Explore can reasonably target 99.9%: if search is briefly down, the app is still usable.

Two practical refinements:

  • Request-based availability is more useful than time-based at this scale: availability = successful requests / valid requests. A 1% error rate for an hour is a partial outage, not "up" or "down".
  • Graceful degradation counts. If ranking is down and we serve a chronological feed, the request succeeded — the user saw a feed. Define "success" carefully.

The maths of how component availabilities combine (series vs parallel) is in Core Principles.

4.3 Latency targets

Latency is the time from request to response. We specify it with percentiles, not averages: "p99 = 300 ms" means 99% of requests finish within 300 ms. Averages hide the slow tail that real users feel — a user who loads 20 feed pages per day will hit the p95 experience about once a day. (Percentiles are explained in depth in Core Principles.)

💡 Latency budget A p99 of 300 ms for the feed must be split across hops: e.g. gateway 10 ms + feed service logic 30 ms + Redis timeline read 5 ms + parallel hydration of 20 posts from cache 40 ms + ranking 80 ms + serialization 10 ms, leaving headroom. If one hop needs 200 ms (a database query), the budget is blown — which is exactly why the feed is pre-computed and cached.

4.4 Durability of media and data

Durability is the probability that data, once acknowledged as stored, is never lost. It is different from availability: a photo can be temporarily unavailable (a region is down) but must never be permanently lost.

  • Media: target object-storage-class durability (S3 is designed for 99.999999999% — "11 nines" — annual object durability). Achieved by replicating or erasure-coding each object across multiple disks, racks and availability zones, plus cross-region copies for disaster recovery. See Object Storage.
  • Acknowledged writes: once the app says "posted", the post must survive any single machine or zone failure. That means the metadata write is replicated (e.g. Cassandra with replication factor 3 and a quorum write) before success is returned — an RPO (recovery point objective: how much data you can lose) of zero for posts.
  • Derived data is re-buildable: timelines in Redis, search indexes and counters can be regenerated from the source of truth, so they need availability more than durability.
  • Deletes must also be durable: when a user deletes content, it must not resurface from a replica or backup restore.

4.5 Consistency expectations per feature

Consistency here means: after a write, what can readers see? Strong consistency (everyone sees the latest write immediately) costs latency and availability, so we pay for it only where users would notice or be harmed. Full theory in CAP, PACELC & Consistency Models.

4.6 Read:write ratio

Instagram is read-heavy, but the ratio depends on what you count:

  • Content views vs content creation: each post is created once and viewed hundreds of times → ~100:1 or more. This is the ratio that justifies pre-computed feeds, caches and CDNs.
  • Bytes out vs bytes in: media egress (tens of PB/day) vs uploads (hundreds of TB/day) → also ~100:1.
  • API requests: likes are writes and very frequent, so at the API level the ratio is lower (perhaps ~3–5:1). That's why the like path is designed as a write-optimised path (append-only store, sharded counters) even inside a read-heavy product.

Saying "read-heavy, ~100:1, so I'll optimise reads — but likes are a high-volume write path that needs special handling" is a strong, nuanced interview statement.

4.7 Security & privacy

  • Authentication & authorization on every request at the API Gateway; tokens with expiry; per-object access checks (private accounts, blocks). See Authentication.
  • Encryption in transit (TLS everywhere, including service-to-service) and at rest (disk/object encryption, managed keys).
  • Media access control: signed, expiring CDN URLs so private photos can't be hot-linked forever (see CDN).
  • Abuse prevention: rate limiting, spam and bot detection, content moderation in the media pipeline.
  • Regulation: data deletion and export (e.g. GDPR "right to erasure"), data residency where required, minors' protections, audit logs.

Details in Security & Privacy.

4.8 Cost

At this scale, cost is a first-class requirement. A design that is 20% cheaper per user saves enormous amounts annually. The big cost drivers are media storage (PBs per year, growing forever), egress bandwidth (CDN), RAM for caches, and compute for transcoding and ML. Cost-shaped decisions include storage tiering (move old, rarely-viewed media to cheaper cold storage), efficient codecs (WebP/AVIF images, modern video codecs), not fanning out to inactive users, and right-sizing caches with the 80/20 rule.

5. Making requirements measurable: SLIs, SLOs and SLAs

"Fast" and "reliable" are not testable. Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) practice turns non-functional requirements into three precise terms:

📏 SLI — Service Level Indicator

A measurement. E.g. "the fraction of feed requests that return 2xx in under 300 ms, measured at the load balancer".

🎯 SLO — Service Level Objective

A target for an SLI over a window. E.g. "99.9% of feed requests in a rolling 28 days are fast and successful".

📜 SLA — Service Level Agreement

A contract with consequences (refunds) if missed. Consumer apps like Instagram don't have SLAs with users; cloud providers and B2B APIs do. SLAs are set looser than internal SLOs.

💰 Error budget

1 − SLO. The amount of unreliability you're allowed. 99.9% → 0.1% of requests may fail. Spend it on deploys and experiments; freeze risky launches when it's exhausted.

An SLO turns "reliable" into a number; the error budget turns that number into a decision-making tool.

⚠️ Measure where the user is An SLI measured inside the feed service ignores failures at the load balancer, DNS or CDN. Prefer measuring at the edge (load balancer logs) or from the client (real-user monitoring). And never set a 100% SLO: it is unachievable, and it leaves zero room to ever deploy anything.

8. How requirements change the design

The best way to understand requirements is to see what happens when one changes. Interviewers love this as a follow-up ("what if…?").

If the requirement becomes……the design changes like this
DMs must be end-to-end encrypted (E2EE)The server can no longer read message content: no server-side search over messages, no server-side spam/content scanning of text, no server-rendered link previews. Clients manage keys (e.g. Signal-protocol style key exchange, per-device keys); multi-device sync and history restore become hard (encrypted backups); moderation shifts to metadata and user reports. The Messages Store holds ciphertext only.
The feed must be strictly chronologicalThe ML ranking stage disappears; the timeline is a simple time-sorted merge. Fan-out still helps, but pull-on-read (merge the latest posts of each followee, k-way merge by timestamp) becomes more attractive because no scoring is needed. "Exactly in order" also demands time-ordered IDs (ID Generation) and consistent clocks.
Like counts must be exact and real-timeAsync counters aren't enough; you need atomic increments on a strongly consistent counter — a hot-key problem for viral posts (millions of likes/hour on one row). Expect sharded counters with synchronous aggregation, higher latency, and lower availability during partitions.
Video-first (like Reels/TikTok)Storage and egress grow ~10×; transcoding compute dominates; adaptive bitrate streaming; the feed becomes a recommendation feed (Explore-like) driven mostly by ML rather than the follow graph.
Only one country, 10M usersOne region; a single primary PostgreSQL with replicas may handle all metadata; simple fan-out; maybe a commercial CDN. Far fewer moving parts — and that's the right answer for that requirement.
Data residency (EU data must stay in the EU)Region-pinned storage per user; routing users to their home region; cross-region features (a German user following a US user) need careful replication of only allowed data.
Posts must appear in followers' feeds within 1 secondFan-out must be fast and prioritised; celebrity pulls at read time; possibly push notifications over persistent connections. Much more infrastructure for marginal user value — worth questioning the requirement.
99.999% availabilityMulti-region active-active for reads and writes, automated regional failover, conflict resolution for concurrent writes, extremely conservative deploys. Cost and complexity increase sharply for each additional nine.
Stories must vanish at exactly 24:00:00Read-time filtering by expires_at (cheap, exact) instead of relying on background TTL deletion (lazy, approximate). CDN URLs must expire too.
💡 Push back on expensive requirements Senior engineers question requirements whose cost exceeds their value: "Do we really need exact like counts? Users see '1.2M' anyway — approximate counts let us avoid a hot-key bottleneck." This is a strong staff-level signal, as long as you accept the interviewer's final decision.

Reading the Instagram diagram through the lens of requirements:

  • The API path and the media path are separate because the latency and bandwidth requirements of the two are completely different — JSON responses are small and personalised (served by services), media is huge and shared (served by the CDN).
  • Kafka sits in the middle because many requirements are "eventually, within seconds" (fan-out, search indexing, notifications, analytics) rather than "before the response". Doing them asynchronously keeps the upload latency target and isolates failures.
  • Different stores for different consistency needs: PostgreSQL where uniqueness and transactions matter (users), Cassandra where write throughput and availability matter (posts, likes), Redis where latency matters (timelines, counters).
  • Stateless services exist because of the availability and scalability requirements: any instance can die or be added without losing data.
📸 In Instagram Instagram's requirements have shifted dramatically over its history, and public engineering talks describe the architecture following them. A famous public example is the feed ordering requirement: Instagram launched with a reverse-chronological feed and moved to a ranked feed in 2016, because as people followed more accounts they were missing a large share of posts. That requirement change brought in an entire ML ranking stack (the "Analytics & ML Training" box). Later, following user demand, Instagram re-introduced "Following" and "Favorites" chronological views as options — the same data, different read-time ordering. Similarly, when Stories launched (2016) the new ephemeral requirement led to TTL-based storage, and the push for end-to-end encrypted messaging across Meta's apps reshaped how DMs are stored. The rough figures used throughout this guide (2B MAU, 500M DAU) are our working assumptions in that spirit — public reports place Instagram's monthly users in the billions.

10. Trade-offs & common pitfalls

✅ Spending time on requirements

  • Prevents designing the wrong thing
  • Gives you numbers to justify every component
  • Lets you choose consistency per feature instead of one-size-fits-all
  • Creates a checklist to verify the final design against
  • Signals product sense and seniority

⚠️ Costs and risks

  • Takes time from design — over 7–8 minutes hurts
  • Too many open questions sound indecisive
  • Over-specifying (exact numbers to 3 significant figures) wastes effort
  • Rigid requirements can hide better options — sometimes the right move is to negotiate them

Common pitfalls

  • Skipping non-functional requirements. Listing features only, then having no basis for choosing caches, replicas or consistency models.
  • "Highly available and strongly consistent everywhere." During a network partition you can't have both (CAP); pick per feature.
  • Averages instead of percentiles. "Average latency 100 ms" says nothing about the 1% of users waiting 3 seconds.
  • Confusing availability with durability. A system can be up and still have lost data, or down with all data safe.
  • Forgetting deletes and privacy. Blocked users, private accounts and legal deletion touch every read path and every cache.
  • Scope creep. Trying to cover DMs, Reels, Explore and ads in 45 minutes. Say "out of scope" explicitly.
  • Treating assumptions as facts. Say "assume 500M DAU", not "Instagram has exactly 500M DAU".
  • Setting a 100% target. Leaves no error budget and is physically impossible.

12. Interview corner

What's the difference between functional and non-functional requirements? Give Instagram examples.

Functional requirements describe behaviour — what a user can do: "upload a photo with a caption", "see a feed of followed accounts", "like a post". Non-functional requirements describe qualities of that behaviour across all users: "feed p99 under 300 ms at 350K requests/s", "99.99% availability", "no acknowledged upload is ever lost", "like counts may be eventually consistent". Functional requirements decide which services and entities exist; non-functional ones decide how they are built — caching, replication, sharding, consistency model, regions.

What does 99.99% availability mean, and how would you achieve it for Instagram's feed?

At most ~52.6 minutes of downtime per year, ~4.4 minutes per month, or 0.01% of requests failing. That's faster than any human can respond, so it requires automation: no single points of failure (redundant load balancers, stateless services across ≥3 availability zones, replicated Redis and databases with automatic failover), health-checked load balancing, gradual deploys with automatic rollback, timeouts and circuit breakers, and graceful degradation — e.g. if ranking fails, serve a chronological timeline from the cache; if the cache fails, serve a smaller feed built from the post store.

Which Instagram features need strong consistency and which can be eventual? Why?

Strong: username uniqueness (duplicates are a correctness bug), authentication and session revocation (security), privacy settings and blocks (privacy harm). Ordered: DMs within a conversation. Read-your-writes: a user's own posts and profile edits. Eventual: home feed, like/view/follower counts, search index, Stories tray — a few seconds of staleness is invisible and buys low latency and high availability at huge write rates.

Why specify latency as p99 rather than average?

Averages hide the tail. If 99% of requests take 50 ms and 1% take 5 s, the average is ~100 ms — it looks fine but one request in a hundred is terrible. Heavy users make many requests per day, so most of them experience the tail daily. Also, a page that calls many backends is slowed by the slowest one, so tail latency of components becomes typical latency of the page. p99 (or p99.9) targets force you to control the tail.

Explain SLI, SLO, SLA and error budget with an Instagram example.

SLI: the measured fraction of feed requests that succeed in under 300 ms. SLO: the target, e.g. 99.9% over 28 days. SLA: an external contract with penalties — Instagram doesn't have one with consumers, but a cloud provider has one with Instagram, and it's looser than internal SLOs. Error budget: 1 − SLO = 0.1% — with ~280B feed requests in 28 days, about 280M may be bad. Teams spend the budget on risk (deploys, experiments); if it's exhausted, they pause launches and fix reliability.

The interviewer says DMs must now be end-to-end encrypted. What changes?

The server only stores and forwards ciphertext. Keys live on devices (per-device identity keys, a Signal-style protocol for session setup), so: multi-device requires encrypting each message for every device of every participant (fan-out per device); message history on a new device needs encrypted backups or device-to-device transfer; server-side features that read content — search over messages, link previews, spam/abuse classifiers on text — move to the client or rely on metadata and user reports. Storage, ordering and delivery mechanics stay similar.

How would you prioritise features when the interviewer says "Design Instagram" with no other guidance?

Propose: core = posting media, following, home feed, like/comment, profiles — the loop that makes Instagram work (create → distribute → consume → engage). Extended = Stories, search, DMs, notifications, Explore — I'll show where they plug in. Out of scope = ads, shopping, payments, live video. Then confirm with the interviewer and ask which part they'd like to dig into. This focuses time on the feed and media pipeline, where the hard scaling problems are.

Is Instagram read-heavy or write-heavy? Be precise.

Read-heavy for content: each post is created once and viewed hundreds of times (~100:1 or more), and media bytes out exceed bytes in by a similar factor — so we optimise reads with timelines in Redis, caches and a CDN. But some paths are write-heavy: likes (~58K/s average under our assumptions), view counters, and the fan-out of each post to hundreds of follower timelines (~20B timeline inserts/day). Those get write-optimised designs: append-only stores, batching, sharded counters.

13. Check your understanding

Classify: (a) "users can delete comments", (b) "comment deletion propagates within 5 seconds", (c) "comments are stored encrypted at rest", (d) "users can reply to a comment".

(a) and (d) are functional (behaviours). (b) is non-functional (a latency/consistency target). (c) is non-functional (security).

Numeric: a service has an SLO of 99.95% availability per 30-day month. How many minutes of downtime is that?

30 days × 1,440 min = 43,200 min. 0.05% of that = 43,200 × 0.0005 = 21.6 minutes per month.

Numeric: with 10B feed loads/day, how many failed requests per day does a 99.99% success SLO allow?

0.01% × 10B = 1 million failed feed requests per day. Large in absolute terms — which is why request-based SLIs are measured as ratios, not counts.

Scenario: your post appears in your followers' feeds 20 seconds after you publish, but on your own profile instantly. Is that a requirements violation?

No. The requirements are read-your-writes for the author's own view (satisfied) and eventual propagation to followers within ~1 minute (satisfied). This is exactly the intended design: the author's write is read from the primary/session, followers get it via asynchronous fan-out.

Why does "username uniqueness" force a different database choice than "like counts"?

Uniqueness requires a linearizable check-and-set: when two people claim the same name simultaneously exactly one must win, which needs a single authority (a leader with a unique constraint, or consensus). Like counts tolerate staleness and approximate values but have a very high write rate, so an AP-leaning, write-optimised store with asynchronous counters fits better.

Which is durability and which is availability: (a) "the photo can't be loaded for 10 minutes during a zone outage", (b) "a disk failure destroyed the only copy of a photo"?

(a) is an availability failure — the data still exists and will come back. (b) is a durability failure — the data is gone forever. Durability is protected with replication/erasure coding across failure domains and backups; availability with redundancy and failover.

The interviewer changes the requirement to "feed must be strictly chronological". Name two components that change.

The ML ranking stage (Analytics & ML / ranking in the News Feed Service) is removed from the read path, and the timeline merge becomes a pure time-ordered merge — which makes pull-on-read (k-way merge of followees' latest posts) more attractive and puts more weight on time-ordered post IDs. Fan-out workers may still be used, but freshness and ordering guarantees become stricter.

Why is "the system must be 100% available" a bad requirement?

It's unachievable (hardware, networks and humans fail), and it leaves an error budget of zero — meaning no deploy, migration or experiment is ever "allowed". Every extra nine costs much more than the last. Choose the lowest target users won't notice and the business can afford.

🎯 Key takeaways
  • Requirements come first: functional = what, non-functional = how well. The second list shapes the architecture far more.
  • Prioritise into core / extended / out of scope; for Instagram the core loop is post → fan-out → feed → like/comment.
  • Quantify: 500M DAU, ~100M posts/day, feed p99 < 300 ms, 99.99% (~52 min/yr), 11-nines media durability, ~100:1 reads.
  • Choose consistency per feature: strong for usernames/auth/privacy, ordered for DMs, read-your-writes for own content, eventual for feeds and counts.
  • Make requirements measurable with SLIs, SLOs and error budgets.
  • Every component should trace back to a requirement — and changing a requirement (E2EE, chronological feed, exact counts) changes the design.
